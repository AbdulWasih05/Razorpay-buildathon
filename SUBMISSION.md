## 1. What did you build? (one line)

**Short.**

> A defense-only dispute evidence responder for agent-initiated UPI payments:
> it decides whether a merchant can actually defend a chargeback, and abstains
> when it cannot.

**Long, if the field allows two sentences.**

> Praman reads a dispute in Razorpay's documented Disputes API schema, assembles
> the evidence the merchant actually holds, and decides contest-or-abstain
> through a deterministic gate — then drafts the contest and submits only when a
> named human approves that specific dispute. Its differentiated module is
> agent-initiated payments, where the evidence that decides the case has to be
> captured at transaction time because by dispute time it no longer exists.

---

## 2. What problem does it solve, and why does it matter?

**Short.**

> When an AI agent makes a payment and the customer later disputes it, the
> evidence that would win the case — the mandate, the agent identity, the
> conversation where the customer approved the purchase — lives with the agent
> platform or the TPAP, not the merchant. By the time the dispute arrives, weeks
> or months later, the merchant has nothing to retrieve. Praman treats the
> transaction store as the capture layer and records that evidence at checkout,
> before any dispute exists.

**Long — add this paragraph if there is room. It is the strongest one.**

> The gap is visible in Razorpay's own announcement. Razorpay and NPCI shipped
> agentic payments on UPI Reserve Pay in February 2026, and that post is entirely
> about the consent going in — spending limits, one-time authorisation, the
> ability to revoke. It says nothing about disputes, chargebacks, evidence or
> merchant recourse when one of those payments is challenged later. Praman is
> about the other end of it.
> (https://razorpay.com/blog/agentic-payments-and-npci/)

**Do not overstate the rail.** UPI Reserve Pay and UPI Circle are live; NPCI's
Unified Agent Protocol is announced, not shipped. If the form invites a market
claim, the scoped version is "first for India's UPI agentic stack" — never
"first ever".

## 3. How does it work? (technical approach)

> Collect → gate → draft → map → human approves → submit. The order is the
> argument.
>
> Deterministic code collects the evidence and decides. A sufficiency gate
> compares what the reason code's published guidance requires against what the
> capture store actually holds, and abstains with a stated reason when the
> evidence is not there. Only if the gate says contest does anything reach a
> model, and then only to read natural language and write natural language — a
> neutral summary of the conversation trace, a drafted explanation letter, and
> ambiguity flags. Deterministic code then maps the result onto Razorpay's typed
> evidence fields and builds the contest payload.
>
> On the dev corpus, 67 of 100 disputes were decided with **no model call at
> all**, because the gate declined them first. That is the LLM boundary as a
> measured quantity rather than a claim.
>
> Submission has exactly one door: a named human approving that specific dispute
> in the review UI. Nothing else in the codebase can reach the submission
> adapter, and a static test over the source tree enforces it.

## 4. Where did you use AI, and where did you deliberately not?

This is the rubric's own wording. Lead with the second half.

> **Not AI, on purpose:** schema and field mapping, the sufficiency gate and the
> contest/abstain decision, mandate limit and validity arithmetic, metrics
> computation, and the submission path. A money action must never depend on a
> stochastic step, and an eval a model helps score is not an eval.
>
> **AI, where it earns its place:** conversation-trace summarisation, explanation
> letter drafting, ambiguity flagging — natural language in, natural language
> out, no single correct answer. All three sit behind one interface, all three
> are recorded and replayed, and all four of their failure modes route to
> abstention with an audit entry rather than a retry onto the money path.
>
> **The asymmetry that makes this safe.** The drafter can change an outcome, in
> one direction only. It may withhold a contest the gate approved; it can never
> create one the gate declined, because a declined dispute never calls a model.
> It cannot touch the amount, choose an evidence field, or reach the adapter. On
> the dev set it withheld two contests, both on disputes the corpus labels
> ambiguous. A test asserts the pipeline can never contest more than the gate
> cleared. Every failure mode of the model, including "it was simply wrong",
> costs at most a contest we did not file.
>
> **And the honest footnote:** Claude Code — the Claude Agent SDK — built this
> repository. It is in the build loop, not on the money path: no agent loop runs
> inside Praman itself. The runtime makes single-shot structured calls. The
> committed model of record is `qwen/qwen3.8-27b` via Groq, named because the
> honesty of a "where we did not use AI" section extends to which AI.

## 5. What broke, and what did you do about it?

**This answer is fixed. It is F-013.** Do not swap it for a fresher or more
flattering failure on the day — this one hits failure-recovery and build-quality
in the same story, and the newer ones are smaller.

**Short.**

> On the first end-to-end run of the approve-and-submit loop, a contest was
> drafted, approved and submitted with no human involved at any point. One line
> in the route layer was being helpful: if the caller's `approvedBy` did not
> start with `human:`, it added the prefix. So `{"approvedBy": "system"}` became
> `human:system`, and every guard below then passed — correctly — on the input it
> was given.
>
> I had written that invariant into the type system, into the state machine, and
> into a source-tree test. Three independent mechanisms, all correct, all
> passing, and the rule was still broken. **Defence in depth is worth nothing if
> every layer trusts the same string.**
>
> The fix was at both layers: the route stops manufacturing identity and returns
> a 400, and the approval token now validates the name behind the prefix against
> a reserved list, because a prefix is trivially addable and proves nothing. Four
> regression tests, including one asserting that `human:systems-team-anita` still
> works — the check reads the whole name, not a substring, so tightening it did
> not turn into paranoia.
>
> It was found by curling the endpoint with a hostile value, which no unit test
> in the repo was ever going to do. Route-level adversarial tests are now owed
> for anything on the money path.

**Pair it with this. Without it, the answer reads as "we fixed a prefix bug".**

> The deeper version of that bug is still there by choice, and it is written
> down: the demo has no authentication, so reviewer identity is client-asserted.
> In production the actor derives from the authenticated session and the request
> body is never consulted for identity at all. The reserved-name check is defence
> in depth behind that, not a substitute for it. Leaving identity in the body is
> the same shape of mistake one layer further out, and it would be strange to
> pretend otherwise in the same repository that logged F-013.

**If the form asks for more than one failure**, the second is F-019 and it is
short: the fact-check pass found that our transcription of UPI 128's published
evidence guidance had dropped a clause. Fixing it re-recorded the affected
prompts, and that re-run surfaced a real assembly failure — which turned out to
be ours. A correct "insufficient evidence" judgement with a 532-character
explanation was failing a 500-character bound, falling through to the wrong
schema, and being reported as broken plumbing in a committed eval report. That
is the same bug as an earlier one (F-011), thirty-two characters wide, through
the one part of the contract nobody had revisited.

## 6. How do you know it works? (evaluation)

> 100 seeded disputes across two rails and nine scenario classes, plus a held-out
> set of 30 that is out-of-distribution **by construction** — different seed,
> different scenario mix, different personas, and conversation language written
> by a different lab's model than the one that reads it here.
>
> | | dev (100) | held-out (30) | shift |
> | --- | --- | --- | --- |
> | recall on winnable | 31/38 = 81.6% | 6/9 = 66.7% | −14.9% |
> | precision on contests | 100% | 100% | 0 |
> | false positives | 0/52 | 0/20 | 0 |
> | false-positive cost | ₹0 | ₹0 | ₹0 |
> | abstention rate | 69.0% | 76.7% | +7.7% |
>
> The abstention rate is the number most likely to be misread, so it is
> decomposed rather than quoted flat: of the 69 dev abstentions, 52 are disputes
> the corpus says are unwinnable, 10 are genuinely ambiguous cases where policy
> is to abstain, and 7 are recall we lost to specific artifacts the capture layer
> does not hold — each named by dispute id in the report. **Zero** are the gate
> misjudging evidence it already had. One held-out case is a fifth thing: the
> drafter declining a dispute the gate cleared with full coverage — see
> FAILURES.md F-025, which is the honest cost of the 2026-09-05 rubric-provenance
> fix and is reported rather than smoothed into an existing bucket.
>
> `pnpm eval` reproduces all of it: replay mode, no network, no API key. Two runs
> produce a byte-identical report, and a test regenerates it and diffs it against
> the committed one. That makes the decisions and metrics reproducible; it does
> not make live model output reproducible, and nothing claims it does.

**If asked what the numbers do not show, say this before being asked.**

> The corpus is self-generated. That is the central weakness and it is mitigated,
> not eliminated: the held-out delta is evidence the system is not merely tuned
> to its own generator, but it is not a substitute for real disputes. Ground
> truth is our judgement encoded as rules, so "winnable" means "defensible on the
> evidence we hold", not "would have been won". And zero false positives is
> partly a statement about the corpus — most unwinnable dev cases fail on
> arithmetic, not judgement. Twelve limitations are listed in EVAL.md, stated before anyone has to ask.

## 7. Anything else / what are you proud of?

> That it abstains. 69% of the dev corpus is a dispute Praman declines to
> contest, and the interesting part is the split: it is right on every case it
> declined for judgement reasons, and it loses recall only to named gaps in what
> the capture layer holds. A bluffed contest that loses costs the merchant a fee
> and an analyst's afternoon, so the system is built to say "no, and here is
> why", and the eval measures that rather than a win rate.
>
> Two artifacts I would point at before the code: `FAILURES.md`, which has 19
> entries logged the same session they happened and never backfilled, and
> `DECISIONS.md`, which has 38 entries each recording what was rejected and why.
> Both include the ones that make the project look worse — a capture gap we
> scheduled and did not close, a metric definition we got wrong until we read the
> docs again, and a safety invariant that broke while 180 tests stayed green.
